The differences between pre-workouts show up in the ingredient amounts, not in the label design or the bold adjectives in the product name. Here's how to read the label, and how to use what you buy without overdoing it.

First question: stim or stim-free?

Pre-workouts fall into two groups. Stimulant products get most of their effect from caffeine. Stim-free products drop the caffeine and keep ingredients such as citrulline and beta-alanine.

The choice depends on when you train. Caffeine's half-life is commonly given as around five hours, varying a lot between people, so an evening dose can still be active at bedtime. If you train in the morning or midday, that concern mostly goes away; if you train in the evening, a stim-free product or a smaller dose is worth considering. Sleep is a big part of what you get from training.

Look at the caffeine amount, not "extreme formula"

The Supplement Facts panel lists caffeine in milligrams per serving, and it varies widely: some products are around a cup of coffee, others far higher. Words like "extreme" aren't a measurement.

Caffeine can also appear in several forms in one product: caffeine anhydrous, green tea extract, guarana. Count all of them. For reference, the FDA cites 400 mg a day as an amount not generally associated with negative effects in healthy adults, and that total includes your coffee, tea, energy drinks and any fat burner.

Beware of proprietary blends

Some products list ingredients as a single "proprietary blend: 8,000 mg" instead of giving each amount. That hides how much of each ingredient you get: most of the blend can be a cheap ingredient while the one on the front of the label is present in a tiny amount. Products that list every amount can be compared; blends can't.

Common ingredients and realistic expectations

Caffeine: the best-known ingredient for alertness and perceived effort, and the source of most of what you feel.

Beta-alanine: the ingredient that causes tingling. The tingle is a known side effect, not a sign that it's working. Beta-alanine also works through daily use over weeks rather than a single dose, so getting it only from a pre-workout on training days gives an inconsistent intake. Our beta-alanine dosage calculator covers the common daily range.

Citrulline / citrulline malate: associated with the "pump." Amounts vary a lot between products, so check the grams per serving.

Tyrosine, theanine: added for focus or to take the edge off caffeine.

Does it contain creatine?

Some pre-workouts include creatine. If you already take creatine, count the total. Creatine works through daily use, so getting it only from a training-day pre-workout doesn't give a steady intake; taking the two separately is usually better for dose control and cost.

Compare price per serving, not tub price

Tubs are sold as "30 servings" or "45 servings," but serving sizes differ. The right comparison is the cost of one serving and how much of the ingredients you care about that serving contains. A product with many servings may need two scoops to reach a meaningful dose.

How long before training?

A common approach is 20-30 minutes before your workout, since caffeine levels in the blood usually peak within 30-60 minutes. Treat it as a starting point and adjust based on how you respond.

Full scoop or half?

If you're new to a product, starting with half a scoop is common advice. Scoops are sized for regular users, and the caffeine can be well above a cup of coffee. Taking a full dose before you know your tolerance can mean a racing heart, jitters or an upset stomach.

A simple habit: on pre-workout days, skip or cut the coffee before training. That one change removes most "it was too much" experiences.

Empty stomach?

On an empty stomach the effect comes on faster, but stomach upset is more likely, especially at higher doses. A light snack beforehand is more comfortable for most people; a heavy meal delays the effect.

Should you use it every workout?

Caffeine tolerance builds over time, so the same dose does less. Using a pre-workout every session speeds that up. Many people save it for their hardest sessions, which slows tolerance and makes the tub last longer. For a second session on the same day, a stim-free option or nothing is usually more sensible than another full dose.

Regulation and third-party testing

In the US, dietary supplements aren't approved by the FDA before they're sold. Stay away from products with unidentifiable ingredients or stimulants the FDA has warned about, such as DMAA. A label should list the manufacturer, a lot number and a best-by date. If you compete in a tested sport, look for certifications such as NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Choice.

Start small

Responses to pre-workouts vary more than for most supplements: the same product can feel fine to one person and cause palpitations or anxiety in another. Try a single-serve packet or small tub before buying the biggest size.

This isn't medical advice

If you have a heart condition, high blood pressure, an anxiety disorder, take medication, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to a doctor before using stimulant products.
