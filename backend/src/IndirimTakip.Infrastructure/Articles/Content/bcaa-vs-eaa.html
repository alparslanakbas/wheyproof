Amino acids are the most confusing part of the supplement shelf: BCAAs, EAAs, single amino acids and dozens of combinations. Understanding the difference often makes it easier to decide what you don't need to buy.

The basic difference

There are nine essential amino acids your body can't make, so they have to come from food. EAA products contain all nine. BCAAs cover only three of them: leucine, isoleucine and valine, the "branched-chain" group.

So BCAAs are a subset of EAAs. That one sentence explains most of the debate between the two.

Why BCAAs were so popular

When leucine's role in triggering muscle protein synthesis got attention, products focused on those three amino acids took off. The later discussion was that starting synthesis isn't enough: sustaining it needs the other essential amino acids too. With an incomplete set, the process is limited by whatever is in shortest supply.

If you eat enough protein, you may need neither

This is the most overlooked point. Whey protein already contains all nine essential amino acids and is rich in BCAAs. So do meat, eggs, dairy and, in combination, legumes.

If you already meet your daily protein target, there's no strong case that adding an amino acid supplement brings extra benefit. The first question to ask is "is my protein intake enough?" If it isn't, closing that gap with food or protein powder is usually more effective and cheaper than amino acid powders.

When they can make sense

There are a few scenarios: training fasted, eating a plant-based diet and struggling with protein variety, or having to keep protein low under a tight calorie budget. In these cases an EAA product can be a bridge. Otherwise, real food or protein powder is usually the more sensible route.

What to check on the label

BCAA products show ratios like "2:1:1" or "4:1:1," the ratio of leucine to isoleucine to valine. 2:1:1 is the most common and the ratio used in most research. A clear advantage for higher leucine ratios hasn't been shown, so paying extra for "8:1:1" is hard to justify.

For EAA products, check that all nine amino acids are listed and how much each serving contains in total. Some products are sold as "EAA" but contain several of them in very small amounts.

Real amino acid content in flavored products

As with creatine, a flavored product's weight isn't all amino acids: sweeteners, flavors and acidity regulators make up part of it. Compare grams of amino acids per serving, not tub weight. Our EAA dosage calculator shows how long a product lasts at a given daily amount.

When to take them

Before or during training is common practice, but there's no strong evidence that timing is decisive. Here too, total daily protein intake matters much more.

What about glutamine, arginine and citrulline?

These are sold for different reasons and each has its own debate. What they share: none replaces your daily protein target, and none is a basic need. Get the fundamentals right first; otherwise each extra product sits on an incomplete foundation.

How to compare prices

Tub price is misleading because servings and grams per serving vary. The right measure is cost per serving together with grams of amino acids in that serving. If two products cost the same and one delivers twice the amino acids per serving, the real price difference is twofold.
