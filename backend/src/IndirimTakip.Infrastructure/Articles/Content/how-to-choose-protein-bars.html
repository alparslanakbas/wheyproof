Protein bars, protein cookies, protein chips, granola: what these products share is being marketed as healthy. Reading the label quickly shows how much of that claim holds up.

Protein bar or candy bar?

Sometimes less different than you'd think. The test is simple: grams of protein per bar and grams of sugar per bar. A 60 g bar with 20 g of protein and little sugar is a protein bar; one with 8 g of protein and 20 g of sugar is a dessert with some protein added.

The second kind isn't necessarily bad, but judge its price and its place accordingly. Paying several times the price of a regular candy bar for a similar nutrition profile doesn't make sense.

Sugar alcohols and "no added sugar"

Many products replace sugar with sugar alcohols such as maltitol or erythritol. These have fewer calories than sugar and let the front of the pack say "no added sugar." Two things are worth knowing.

First, calories: most sugar alcohols aren't calorie-free, just lower in calories. Second, digestion: in some people, especially in larger amounts, they cause bloating or discomfort. The amount you tolerate in one bar may not be the amount you tolerate in two.

The ingredient order tells you a lot

Ingredients are listed by weight. If syrup, sugar or vegetable oil comes before the protein source, that's what the product is mostly made of. "High protein" may be printed large on the front, but the ingredient order is more reliable.

Don't ignore calorie density

These products pack a lot of calories into a small size. That's an advantage if you're trying to gain weight and a trap if you're in a calorie deficit. A bar can run 200-400 calories while feeling less filling than a real meal with the same calories.

The practical takeaway: count a bar as part of your daily calorie budget, not as an innocent snack.

When they're genuinely useful

Their real value is convenience. On the road, at work or traveling, when a proper meal isn't available, a bar can be a better protein source than most alternatives around you. At home, with eggs or Greek yogurt in the fridge, a bar is usually both more expensive and nutritionally weaker.

Peanut butter and nut butters

The simplest product in this category, but labels still vary. A jar whose ingredients are just peanuts (and maybe salt) is a different product from one with added sugar, palm oil and emulsifiers. Reading the ingredient list is the fastest way to tell them apart.

Rice cakes and oat products

These are mainly carbohydrate sources with little protein; the "healthy" image usually comes from being low in fat. They're a reasonable quick carb before training, but they don't cover protein needs. Coated versions (chocolate, yogurt) add noticeably more sugar.

The real comparison: cost per gram of protein

A bar's price tells you little on its own. What would the same 20 g of protein cost from protein powder? Usually noticeably less. Doing that math makes clear what the bar's price pays for: convenience and taste, not protein.

That's not a reason to skip bars; convenience is a real need. Knowing where the money goes just lets you choose deliberately.

Allergens

Most of these products contain milk, soy or nuts, or are made in facilities that process them. If you have an allergy, read the allergen statement on the label; "may contain" warnings matter for serious allergies.
