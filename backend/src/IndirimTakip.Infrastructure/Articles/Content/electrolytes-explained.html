Electrolyte powders, tablets and drink mixes have become a big category. Some people genuinely benefit from them; many others are paying for flavored water. Here's how to tell which group you're in.

What electrolytes are

Electrolytes are minerals that carry an electrical charge in body fluids. They help regulate fluid balance, nerve signals and muscle contraction. The ones you'll see on labels are mainly sodium, potassium, magnesium and sometimes calcium and chloride.

When you sweat, you lose water and electrolytes, and sodium is the one lost in the largest amount. That's why sodium is the ingredient that matters most in a product meant to replace what sweat takes out.

Do you actually need them?

For a typical gym session of an hour or less in normal conditions, water is usually enough, and the food you eat during the day replaces the electrolytes you lost.

Electrolyte products make more sense when:

  • you train or compete for long periods, especially endurance sessions well beyond an hour;
  • you exercise in heat or humidity and sweat heavily;
  • you're a "salty sweater" (white salt marks on clothes or skin after training);
  • you train more than once a day, with little time to eat in between.

How much sodium people lose in sweat varies widely from person to person, so a product that suits a teammate may be too much or too little for you.

More water isn't always the answer

During very long events, drinking large amounts of plain water without replacing sodium can dilute the sodium in your blood, a condition called hyponatremia. It's uncommon but serious, and it's one of the real reasons endurance athletes use sodium-containing drinks. For everyday training, simply drinking to thirst is a sensible guide. How to read the label

Sodium per serving: the most important number. Products range from barely any sodium to a lot; a mix with very little sodium does little to replace sweat losses.

Potassium and magnesium: useful, but sweat contains much less of them than sodium, so a product that leads with potassium or magnesium while being low in sodium is aimed at something other than sweat replacement.

Sugar: some drink mixes contain significant sugar. During long endurance sessions, carbohydrate is actually useful fuel; for a short gym session or all-day sipping, it's mostly extra calories. Zero-sugar products usually use sweeteners instead.

Serving size and servings per container: as always, compare per serving, not per tub.

Tablets, powders or ready-to-drink?

The form mainly affects convenience and cost. Tablets and single-serve sticks are easy to carry; tubs of powder are usually cheapest per serving; ready-to-drink bottles are the most expensive per serving. The electrolyte content is what you're paying for, so compare cost per serving alongside sodium per serving.

Everyday "hydration" products

Many products are marketed for daily hydration at a desk rather than for training. If you're not sweating heavily, most people get enough sodium and potassium from food; these products are a flavor and habit choice more than a nutritional need. That's fine, as long as you know what you're paying for.

Who should be careful

Electrolyte products can add a meaningful amount of sodium to your day. If you have high blood pressure, kidney disease, heart failure, or you're on a sodium- or potassium-restricted diet or take medication that affects electrolytes, talk to your doctor before using them regularly.

This isn't medical advice

Symptoms such as confusion, severe headache, vomiting or muscle cramps that don't go away during or after exercise need medical attention, not another drink mix.
